The short version

A Random Forest predicting regional PM2.5 from the IQAir 2025 report at R² ≈ 0.97.

That number is smaller than it looks, for three documented reasons — §04. And two claims on my CV that this repo does not support — §05.

01

The question

Air quality is measured well in some places and barely at all in others. Karachi, where I live, sits in a region that appears in global rankings mostly as a warning. The practical question behind this project: given the environmental variables a region does report, how well can you predict its PM2.5 concentration?

PM2.5 is the fraction worth predicting — particles under 2.5 micrometres, small enough to reach deep into the lungs, and the measure most air-quality health guidance is built around.

02

Data and method

DecisionWhat I didWhy
Dataset IQAir 2025 World Air Quality Report, released March 2026 — global monitoring data for the 2025–2026 cycle. Published, citable, and covers regions that rarely appear in cleaner academic datasets.
Framing Regression on a continuous PM2.5 target, not classification into AQI bands. Bands throw away information, and the band boundaries are policy choices rather than physical ones.
Models A comparative framework across two ensemble families — Random Forest and Gradient Boosting. Both handle non-linear, mixed-scale tabular features without heavy preprocessing. Comparing two is how you learn whether the result is the method or the data.
Structure Separate training, prediction and diagnostic pipelines; dependencies pinned in requirements.txt under a virtualenv. So a reader can rerun the training without rerunning my exploration.
03

Result

The Random Forest reached R² ≈ 0.97. In plain terms: across the regions in the dataset, the model accounts for about 97% of the variation in PM2.5.

What R² ≈ 0.97 is not

It is not "97% accuracy". Accuracy is a classification metric and this is a regression — there is no notion of a prediction being right or wrong here, only near or far. I have written "97% accuracy" myself in a hurried draft, and it is the kind of error a reviewer in this field spots instantly.

It is also not a claim about causation, and not a promise the model travels. Both of those get their own section below, because they are the honest limits of the work.

04

What I would not claim

Three limitations, all of them documented in the repository rather than discovered by a reviewer:

Correlation, not causality

A high R² means the model learned patterns that co-occur with high PM2.5. It does not mean those variables cause the pollution. Nothing here supports a policy recommendation of the form "change X and PM2.5 falls" — that needs a causal design this project does not have.

The accuracy is unevenly distributed

Predictive robustness concentrates in densely monitored regions — South Asia, Europe, North America. Those places have the monitoring stations, so they dominate the data the model learned from. For a sparsely monitored region the honest expectation is materially worse than R² ≈ 0.97, and the headline figure hides that.

It is specialised to one reporting cycle

The findings belong to the 2025 cycle. Monitoring coverage changes year to year, and so does which regions are represented at all. This is a snapshot model, not a forecaster.

05

Where the record disagrees with itself

Two claims on my CV that this repository does not support

My Europass CV states MAE = 3.13 µg/m³ and says the model "served real-time inferences through a modular FastAPI framework". The repository documents neither. It reports R² ≈ 0.97 and describes local execution through Python scripts — there is no MAE figure and no serving layer in it.

Both may well be true of work I did and never wrote down. But until the repo shows them, the repo is what a reader can check, so this page claims only R² ≈ 0.97. Fixing that mismatch — either by publishing the serving code and the MAE, or by correcting the CV — is on the still-ugly list.

I am putting this on the page rather than quietly picking the flattering version, because the entire outreach pipeline I built exists to catch exactly this class of drift between what I claim and what I can show. It would be strange to run that pipeline on job postings and not on my own portfolio.

06

What I would do next

In order of what would most improve the work:

  1. Publish a predicted-vs-actual plot. One scatter with the diagonal shows where the model fails in a way R² cannot.
  2. Report MAE and RMSE alongside R². R² is a proportion of variance; error in µg/m³ is what a person can reason about.
  3. Hold out a region, not a random sample. Random splits let neighbouring rows leak into each other. Holding out an entire region tests the thing I actually care about — does it generalise to somewhere it has not seen?
  4. Publish the Gradient Boosting comparison. The framework compares two families; only one number is written down.
