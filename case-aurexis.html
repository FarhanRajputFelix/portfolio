AUREXIS — keeping someone alive when the systems don't

A software-defined survival habitat architecture for humanity's most extreme frontiers — sub-orbital habitats, deep-sea stations, disaster zones. Five specialised agents under a Meta-Arbitrator, built around the assumption that the occupant may be unconscious, panicking, or unable to operate anything at all.

The short version

Five agents under a Meta-Arbitrator keeping someone alive when they cannot act. Simulated survival 15.0% → 55.8% across 10,000 Monte Carlo trials.

Those are results from a simulation I wrote myself — §04. What is built versus only designed is §03.

01

The premise: assume the human cannot help

Most safety software assumes an operator who reads the alert and acts. In the environments AUREXIS targets, that assumption is exactly the one that fails first — the occupant may be hypoxic, injured, asleep or panicking.

So the organising idea is the Zero-Interaction Protocol: survival should not depend on anyone pressing anything. The system is designed to keep an occupant alive through the window in which they can contribute nothing.

02

Five agents, one arbitrator

Five specialised agents coordinate through a Meta-Arbitrator (aurexis_orchestrator.py) that resolves them by priority — because in an emergency the agents genuinely conflict, and something has to decide.

AgentModuleWhat it does
Environment · ER-AIworld_model.pyDetects threats across 10 categories via thresholds.
Resource · RM-AIration_planner.pyDynamic rationing with activity scaling and 15% water recycling.
Health · HH-AIvitals.pyMonitors heart rate and SpO₂ with threshold alerts.
Psychology · PW-AIpanic_detection.pyScores panic from keywords, volume, speech rate and heart rate.
Emergency · ED-AIdistress_ai.pyAutonomous escalation and evacuation logic.

The conflict the arbitrator exists for is real: the Resource agent wants to ration oxygen, the Health agent sees SpO₂ falling, and the Psychology agent reads rising panic — which itself raises oxygen consumption. Three correct local decisions that contradict each other. A priority ordering is a blunt resolution, but it is explicit and inspectable, which matters more here than being clever.

It is rule-based, and that is deliberate

These agents are thresholds and rules, not neural networks. For life-critical logic that is a feature: the behaviour is auditable, deterministic and explainable to someone deciding whether to trust it. Physics-informed neural networks and reinforcement learning are listed as future work, not as something already in place.

04

The numbers, and what they are worth

Across 10,000 Monte Carlo trials with seed 2026 for reproducibility, against a baseline with no coordinating system:

MeasureBaselineAUREXISDelta
Survival rate15.0%55.8%+40.8 pp
Mean survival time4.9 hrs7.8 hrs+2.9 hrs
Resource isolation durationstatic rationing1.45×longer
These are simulation results, and the simulation is mine

The +40.8 percentage point improvement is measured inside a model I wrote, against a baseline I also defined. It is evidence that the coordination logic beats no coordination under my assumptions about how a habitat fails — not evidence that anyone would survive a real hull breach.

The honest reading: the number tells you the architecture is internally coherent and worth continuing. It tells you nothing about physical reality until the hardware exists and someone tests it. Seed 2026 is fixed so the figures reproduce — reproducible is not the same as validated.

05

Where it goes

The gap between this and real research is an external validator. Next, in order: build the FPGA safety layer so the hardware override stops being a diagram; deploy to a Raspberry Pi and run the agents against real sensors; and get the failure model itself reviewed by someone who works on life-support systems, because that model is the single assumption every number above rests on.

It evolved from an earlier C-language prototype (MESP) and is under active development as a research prototype.
