01

One-line claim

I build AI that knows its limits — and ships anyway.

Chosen because it is the one thing my work actually proves rather than asserts. The agent on my site refuses questions outside its knowledge base instead of inventing an answer; my AQI model reports a real R² instead of a vibe; my hero says “CS student” rather than “AI researcher” because I have no publications yet. The line also survives being said out loud, which is the test I used to cut the other nine.

The ten options, and why this one won

01“Computer Science undergraduate passionate about AI, ML and Data Science.” — Rejected: that is a LinkedIn headline, not a claim. Says nothing only I could say.
02“I turn messy real-world data into systems people can trust.” — Rejected: good shape, but “trust” is unproven by a student with no users yet.
03“AI for classrooms, clinics and crises.” — Close. Memorable and true to KIDO / health interest / Crisis Intelligence. Kept as the About sub-line instead.
04“I ship AI products, from the model to the interface.” — Rejected: true but generic; every bootcamp graduate writes this.
05“R² 0.97 on air quality, an AI tutor for kids, an agent that refuses to guess.” — Rejected as a claim, promoted to evidence: it is three facts, not one sentence.
06“I build AI that knows its limits — and ships anyway.” — Picked. Specific, provable on the page itself, and it says something most portfolios avoid saying.
07“Every claim on this site links to proof.” — Rejected: describes the site, not me. Became the certifications section's job instead.
08“A CS student building AI systems that hold up under scrutiny.” — Rejected: 11 words to say what option 06 says with more edge.
09“Ship first, publish later.” — Rejected: catchy but reads as dismissive of research, which is the opposite of my goal.
10“I make AI useful where it matters most.” — Rejected: “matters most” is a claim about the world, not about my work.

Sharpening step: option 06 started as “I build AI that admits what it doesn't know.” “Admits” sounded apologetic, so it became “knows its limits”, and “— and ships anyway” was added so the line ends on capability instead of caution.

How every CTA ladders to the one action

Hero — “See my work”→gets them into the Work section
Work — “Ask BYTE about any project”→answers objections without me in the room
Case study — “Open the notebook / repo”→converts interest into verified belief
Certifications — “Verify ↗”→removes the “is this real?” doubt
Process pages — “Back to portfolio”→returns a curious reader to the work
Contact — “Write email ↗”→the one action: an email in my inbox

Rule I am holding myself to: one filled violet button per screen. Secondary actions are outlined or plain text, so the eye always finds the step that matters.
