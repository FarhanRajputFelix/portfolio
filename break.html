Break your own site · Week 9

I attacked my own portfolio

Not by clicking around — by writing a script that tries every link, feeds the contact form garbage, submits five times in a row, walks path traversal at the server, and audits the metadata on all nineteen pages. tools/break-it.py, run against the live site.

If you read one thing

Two real bugs. Every page had zero social-share metadata, so pasting my link into LinkedIn rendered a blank rectangle — on a site whose whole job is being shared with people who might hire me. And the contact form 404s on roughly one submission in five.

First run: 24 failures. Now: 0 real ones. The form 404 is Netlify's, not mine, so it has a mitigation and stays on the list rather than being called solved.

02

Fix now — and fixed

1 · No social-share metadata on any pageFixed

Broken
All 19 pages missing Open Graph and Twitter card tags. Pasting my portfolio into LinkedIn, WhatsApp or Slack produced a blank grey rectangle with a bare URL. For a site whose entire purpose is being shared with people who might hire me, this was the worst thing on the list — and completely invisible from the site itself.
Fixed
Full Open Graph and Twitter metadata on every page, each carrying its own title and description rather than one generic pair. Plus a generated 1200×630 share card that states the claim and carries three pieces of proof, because a card with only a name on it wastes the one impression you get.

2 · robots.txt returned 404Fixed

Broken
No robots.txt at all. Not fatal — crawlers assume permission — but it meant nothing pointed them at my sitemap, and the form success page was crawlable.
Fixed
Written, with /thanks.html disallowed and the sitemap declared. Also added JSON-LD Person schema so a search engine can state who I am rather than infer it from the markup.

3 · The form 404s intermittentlyMitigated

Broken
Netlify's form endpoint returns 404 on roughly one submission in five. Not rate limiting — reproduced at three-second spacing with no deploy running: 200, 200, 200, 200, 404, 200. A visitor who hits it sees an error page and almost certainly never tries again, so the site silently loses the one action it exists to produce.
Mitigated
js/form.js intercepts the submit, posts with fetch, and retries up to three times with backoff. If all three fail it says so and offers my email address rather than dropping the message into a void. It also disables the button while sending, which handles the double-click the same testing found.
Why "mitigated"
Because the 404 is still there. It is Netlify's endpoint and I cannot fix it — I can only stop it costing me a message. Calling that "fixed" would be the kind of claim this whole site argues against. It stays on the list below too.

04

Known limitations — named, not hidden

The form 404 is still thereOpen

Retries hide it from visitors with JavaScript. Someone with JavaScript disabled still gets a raw 404 roughly one time in five, and the no-JS path is the one I deliberately built the form around. I have no fix for that and will not pretend otherwise.

Time to first byte varies 690 ms to 1,275 msKnown

Measured twice, minutes apart, from Karachi. That is a cold edge cache on a free tier, and it is the number I would attack next if I were optimising. Not measured with Lighthouse yet — my own still-ugly list has said the 3D scene is unmeasured for two weeks now, and that remains true.

Three external links refuse automated checksNot a break

LinkedIn returns 999, its well-known anti-scraping code. Welcome to the Jungle returned 403 to my script but 202 with a browser user-agent — verified, so it works for real visitors. IndiaBioscience returns 403 even with a browser user-agent from here, which I have not resolved; it may be geographic. Recorded as unresolved rather than assumed fine.

The form only works on this hostBy design

My GitHub Pages copy renders identical markup with no endpoint behind it. Pages serves files and runs no code.

Free tier caps at 100 submissions a monthUpgraded — see below

I filed this as a mild ceiling. The hardening review reclassified it as a denial-of-service on my only conversion path, and was right: nothing throttles a script POSTing straight past the honeypot. Reasoning and current status in the review section below.

06

The hardening review — and what it overturned

I sent the list above out for review with one question: what did I miss, and is anything I called a known limitation really a must-fix? Both halves got answered, and the second half is why it was worth asking.

1 · "That's not a ceiling, that's a denial-of-service"Upgraded to must-fix

On the 100-submissions-a-month cap, which I had filed as a mild known limitation:

"Your honeypot only catches bots dumb enough to fill a hidden field. Anything smarter — or just a scraper hitting the endpoint directly, the way your own break-it.py does — submits clean, blank-honeypot garbage that counts against your quota. There's nothing between 'form is public' and 'form is exhausted'. Someone sending 100 junk POSTs silently takes your contact form offline for the rest of the month, with zero signal to you. That's not 'a ceiling', that's a denial-of-service on the one conversion path the site has."

They are right, and my own test script is the proof. break-it.py POSTs straight to the endpoint and never touches the honeypot — I built the attack that defeats my own defence and did not notice, because I read its output as "the form accepts input" rather than "the form accepts anything from anyone, 100 times, then dies quietly".

The measured picture is better than the worst case, though. Across two full break-it runs, the sequential test and the rapid-fire test I sent roughly 30 POSTs at that endpoint. The dashboard records 8 verified submissions. About 22 were filtered before they counted — Netlify's own spam filtering is automatic, which is why there is no toggle to enable, and the "Verified submissions" heading exists precisely because a filtered bucket sits behind it. So junk POSTs do not map one-to-one onto quota the way the worst case assumes.

That lowers the risk. It does not remove it: a script that sends plausible-looking submissions would pass the same filter, and 100 of those still ends the month.

Status: partially addressed, and honestly so. Netlify's built-in Akismet spam filtering is the recommended next step and is a dashboard setting rather than code. What I cannot do on a free static host is per-IP throttling — there is no server of mine to throttle at. A determined script can still exhaust the quota. The realistic mitigation is that the email address stays visible beside the form, so the conversion path survives the form dying.

2 · I tested where payloads don't go, not where they doFair hit

"You confirmed the payloads are stored inert and never reflected back to the public site. But you didn't check the other consumer of that data — the Netlify submissions dashboard, and the email notification. If either renders the name field as HTML rather than escaping it, an onerror payload could still fire in the notification you receive."

A precise correction. I checked the surface I control and called the payload harmless, when the actual consumer of that data is an inbox I read every day.

Checked, and the finding did not reproduce — but not for the reason I would have guessed. The injection submission never arrived at all. Every other test is in the inbox and renders correctly: the deliberately-empty submissions show empty fields, and the unicode test shows فرحان 🚀 and اردو text 日本語 🎉 exactly as sent. The one carrying <script> and an onerror payload was filtered before delivery.

So the honest status is not reproduced, not proven safe. I cannot demonstrate correct escaping of a payload that was never rendered. What I can say is that no path from that input to my inbox exists today, and that Gmail strips <script> and blocks remote images by default, so two independent layers would have to fail together. Recording it as unresolved-but-unreachable rather than closed, because those are different things.

3 · Response headers were never checkedFixed

"Nothing in the script checks CSP, X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options or HSTS. Doesn't mean they're missing — Netlify sets some sane defaults — but you never verified it, and it's a two-line curl away from being provable instead of assumed."

Checked, and they were missing, not merely unverified. Only HSTS was set; CSP, X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options, Referrer-Policy and Permissions-Policy were all absent. Added via netlify.toml, and break-it.py now checks all six, so this can never be assumed again.

The CSP allows inline script and style, because this site has 16 inline <style> blocks, an importmap and a JSON-LD block. Removing those is a refactor, not a header change. What it still buys: no script may load from an origin I did not name, the site cannot be framed, and forms cannot post anywhere but back to me.

What the review taught me

The two findings I got wrong were the same mistake in different clothes: I tested the surface I was thinking about and declared the whole thing safe. The public page does not render the payload — but the inbox might. The honeypot stops naive bots — but not the attack tool I wrote myself, sitting in my own repository.

Triage was the part I was most confident about and the part that was most wrong. Two of five known limitations were misfiled, and one of them was a quiet denial-of-service on the only conversion path this site has.
